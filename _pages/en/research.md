My research examines the domestic political consequences of economic globalization, focusing on how international economic integration shapes local economic conditions, distributional outcomes, and political competition. I am particularly interested in the connections between **international political economy and comparative politics**, and in how economic transformations influence electoral behavior and the political survival of incumbent governments.

## Doctoral Dissertation

### Foreign Direct Investment: Distributional Consequences and the Electoral Survival of Incumbent Parties

My doctoral dissertation investigates how foreign direct investment (FDI) affects the electoral survival of incumbent parties. While existing research has extensively examined the economic and political determinants of foreign investment, its domestic electoral consequences remain less understood, particularly at the subnational level.

I develop an argument linking productive foreign investment to electoral outcomes through its distributional consequences. Greenfield investments and the expansion of productive capacity can generate employment, transform local labor markets, and affect household economic conditions. These changes may also influence government spending priorities and voters' evaluations of incumbent administrations.

The dissertation examines three interconnected dimensions:

1. **The political determinants of foreign investment:** How political and institutional conditions shape the location and allocation of FDI across subnational territories.

2. **Foreign investment and electoral survival:** Whether and under what conditions FDI contributes to the electoral continuity of incumbent parties, particularly through changes in local economic conditions and employment opportunities.

3. **The distributional and political consequences of investment:** How different types of FDI generate heterogeneous economic effects and shape public spending priorities, voter evaluations, and electoral outcomes.

The empirical analysis focuses primarily on Mexican states, combining quantitative approaches with comparative and qualitative evidence.

## Other Research Interests and Previous Work

Beyond my doctoral research, I have pursued research on topics spanning political history and the political economy of development.

My previous research includes a master's thesis examining the effects of foreign direct investment on total factor productivity across Latin American countries, as well as research notes on the history of the Mexican Revolution. These projects reflect my broader interest in understanding the historical and economic processes shaping political and social development.

My academic background also includes undergraduate research on sudden ionospheric disturbances associated with solar activity, reflecting my earlier training in engineering and scientific research.
